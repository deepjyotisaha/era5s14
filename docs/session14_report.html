Session 14 — Mixture-of-Experts

A model gets better with more parameters, and gets slower when each token has to use all of them. Mixture-of-experts stores many small feed-forward networks and lets each token use only a few. This session covers how that choice is made, how it is kept fair, where the pieces live on GPUs, and how to grow such a model from a dense one.

Part 1 — Session Takeaways

1. Opening: a 16× data finding

Before MoE, the instructor gave an update on the course's own model (V5). Last session's reversibility idea cuts memory. This week they found something that he said lets the team go through far more training data for the same compute, which changes the data target. His rough version: an early phase of training that used to be expensive can be done about 16 times faster, and then roughly the last 25% of training runs at normal cost. He said the data target might move from 5–10 trillion tokens to 20–25 trillion, or more if the budget allows. He did not explain the method and said he will share it when training starts.

Flag — the opening numbers are garbled

The transcript reads "1200 into 1 divided by 16 plus 800 = 875", "at least 60% savings", "16 June tokens can be converted into a one trillion token" and "two thousand billion". These do not fit together: 1200/16 + 800 = 875, which against 2,000 is a 56% saving, and against 1,200 + 800 = 2,000 it is also 56%, not "at least 60%". Treat the message as: a large early part of training becomes ~16× cheaper, the total bill drops by roughly half, and the data target grows. The method was not described, so nothing here can be checked.

He then said all the big labs are believed to use MoE, even though OpenAI and Anthropic do not publish their architectures. That is why this session is "foundational" for V5.

2. Why this session exists: where the parameters live

Recall the transformer layer. Attention mixes information between tokens. Then a feed-forward network (FFN) works on each token alone: it widens the token's vector (for example 1,024 to 4,096), applies a non-linearity, and narrows it back. In the reference model this FFN would be 6,144 wide, and it holds most of the layer's weights.

An MoE layer keeps attention exactly as it is. It replaces the one FFN with many small FFNs (called experts) and a tiny router that picks a few of them for each token.

Carry forward

MoE changes only the FFN part of each layer. More weights are stored; about the same work is done per token.

3. The three new problems: choosing, balancing, placing

The saving comes with three new jobs, and the rest of the session is organised around them.

He used a family analogy for balancing: a parent who says "I love you all equally" but gives one child more sweets. The router does the same unless something stops it. For placing, the risk is that one GPU receives most of the tokens, needs more memory than it has, and crashes with an out-of-memory error.

4. The whiteboard version, and the "concatenate" confusion

He first drew a small example: hidden size 1,024, 64 experts, the router picks 4, each with a weight such as 0.8, 0.7, 0.9, 0.3. He said each chosen expert gives 256 numbers and the four are "concatenated" to get 1,024 back. A student asked what happens if only two are picked (256 + 256 is not 1,024). He answered that the router must always pick exactly 4.

A few minutes later he corrected himself: "we are not doing concatenation... we take a sum". The page is clear that the output is a weighted sum. This matters for the assignment, so here is the difference.

Flag — concatenation answers in the Q&A

Two answers were given under the concatenation picture and are not right for the actual design. (1) "It's compulsory to select 4" — the real reason k is fixed is balancing and fixed compute, not dimension matching. (2) Late in the session a student said copied experts are "averaged" but partitioned experts are "concatenated", and he agreed. In both cases the experts' outputs are full width and are added with router weights. What differs is only the inner width of each expert. A2 shows why this matters for the assignment.

Other short answers from this Q&A: the router is the thing that replaces the one big FFN (a student asked whether it replaces "SwiGLU"; each expert still uses SwiGLU inside); the goal is more total parameters but fewer active ones; the order of experts does not matter; the number of experts picked is fixed.

5. The reference model, and grouped-query attention

The whole session uses one real shape so every number can be checked: Qwen3-30B-A3B, an open model from 2025. "30B" is total parameters and "A3B" is about 3B active. I checked the numbers below against its published config.json; they match.

Jargon — GQA, grouped-query attention

Every attention head asks its own question (a query, Q). Normally each head also has its own keys and values (K, V). In GQA, groups of query heads share one K/V head. Here 32 ÷ 4 = 8 query heads share each K/V head.

Why share? During generation the model keeps every past token's keys and values in memory (the KV cache). Per token in 16-bit: 2 (K and V) × 4 heads × 128 × 2 bytes × 48 layers = 96 KiB. With 32 KV heads it would be 768 KiB. For one 32,768-token sequence that is 3 GiB instead of 24 GiB. Why 4 and not 1: one shared KV head (multi-query attention) saves the most but loses quality; a few groups keep almost all the quality. Qwen3 uses 4 and Llama 3 uses 8.

One oddity: 32 query heads × 128 = 4,096, which is wider than the hidden size of 2,048. Qwen3 widens for the queries on purpose and the output projection brings it back. That is why attention is 18.87M parameters per layer.

Flag — GQA numbers in the transcript

"128 into 32 gives 4,096, then divide by 4 gives 1,024 back" is not a real step: the K and V widths are 4 × 128 = 512 each. "The GQA paper came in 2025": the page cites Ainslie et al., 2023. "Going to 8 or 16 doesn't help": the page says four to eight groups is the common choice, and Llama 3 uses 8. The page itself has a stray editing note after the GQA block ("I can add a two-sentence note...") that is leftover authoring text, not lesson content.

6. Total parameters and active parameters

An MoE model has two sizes. Total = every weight stored. Active = the weights one token actually uses on its way through. I recomputed the page's count:

Jargon — "6 per active parameter" and "16 bytes per weight"

Training costs about 6 floating-point operations (FLOPs) per active parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward pass. Training memory is about 16 bytes per stored weight in standard mixed precision: 2 for the 16-bit weight, 2 for its gradient, and 12 for the fp32 master copy plus Adam's two moments (from Session 12).

Flag — "28.99 active parameters"

The transcript says "28.99 active parameters". 28.99B is the total held in experts; the active count is 3.35B. He also called 30B-A3B "a 10% kind of model" (3.35/30.53 = 11%) and said people are "pushing to 1.9%". The 1.9% figure comes from DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, discussed in Section 13.

Carry forward

Total decides how many GPUs you need to hold the model. Active decides how long each token takes.

8. The MoE layer, step by step (snake diagram)

Here is one MoE layer drawn in the same "snake" style as the Session 12 forward-pass page. Read left to right, then down, then right to left, and so on. Boxes are operations; the number grids beside them are the data going in and out. Sizes are a toy version so everything fits: 8 experts, top-2, and the token vectors show only 4 of their numbers. The reference model runs the same steps with 128 experts and top-8.

The router matrix was set up so that the token "is" gets exactly the lesson's example logits (2.0, 0.5, 1.2, −0.3, 0.9, 1.8, −1.0, 0.1). Every other number is computed by a small script (tools/make_snake.py), not typed in.

In formula form, with T the chosen set and g the weights: y = Σi∈T gi · Ei(x). Each expert is a SwiGLU FFN with its own three matrices: E(x) = Wdown( SiLU(Wgatex) ⊙ Wupx ), where gate and up are 768 × 2,048 and down is 2,048 × 768.

Two points from the Q&A. The router chooses again for every token in every layer, so one sentence passes through many different experts. And the whole layer still runs for every token, just as the FFN did; only its inside changed. Some models keep their first one to three layers dense because routing there settles slowly; every layer is MoE in the reference model.

Carry forward

Router → scores → keep top k → rescale to add up to 1 → run only those experts → weighted sum.

9. What an expert is

The config file of the reference model lists a standard FFN width of 6,144 and an expert width of 768. 8 × 768 = 6,144. So each token uses exactly one standard FFN's worth of width, assembled from 8 pieces chosen out of 128.

MiMo-V2.6 (Xiaomi, September 2026) follows the same rule: one standard layer would be 16,384 wide, and each token uses 8 experts of 2,048 (8 × 2,048 = 16,384).

An expert is not a subject specialist. Training never assigns "physics" or "cricket" to an expert. He gave the reason: a batch is a random mix of text, and some topics take far more tokens than others, so topics cannot be balanced across experts. What experts do learn is Section 14.

Counting Mixtral 8x7B. 8 experts, each the size of a 7B model's FFN. 8 × 7B would be 56B, but attention and embeddings are shared, so the real total is 47B, and about 45B of that (96%) sits in the experts. In the reference model the experts hold 28.99B of 30.53B, which is 95%.

Carry forward

An expert is one small FFN among many identical ones. The chosen experts together have the width of one normal FFN.

10. Why mixture-of-experts, and what it costs

Quality grows with total parameters; cost per token grows with active parameters. MoE raises the first and keeps the second small. The page's evidence:

studycomparisonresult
Switch Transformer (Google, 2021)64 experts vs a dense model with the same work per tokensame quality, 7× faster
Krajewski et al. (2024)MoE tuned for 1020 FLOPsmatches a dense model given 20× the compute
Ling team (2025)0.85B active of 17.5B totalmatches a 6.1B dense model at over 7× less compute
Kimi K2 (2025)48 experts per active expert vs 8same loss with 1.69× fewer FLOPs

He asked the class to look at the three sizes in the Ling row together: the MoE (17.5B total) is much bigger than the dense model it matches (6.1B), but its active part (0.85B) is much smaller. There is also a theory result (Chen et al., 2022): on data made of separate clusters, one network of a certain kind reaches at most 87.5% accuracy, while a mixture of them reaches nearly 100%, because the router learns the clusters.

The costs: memory follows the total (all 30.53B weights must be stored and trained); communication (DeepSeek-V3 reported about one unit of communication time per unit of compute); stability (Switch diverged when its router ran in 16-bit); fine-tuning (the ST-MoE study found MoE overfits small fine-tuning sets more easily).

Is an MoE as good as a dense model of the same total size? A student asked this. His answer was no: dense is usually a little better at the same total, because every weight sees every token. A student posted that Qwen3.6-27B (dense) beats Qwen3.6-35B (MoE), and he agreed that the gap has narrowed, and said much of the dense model's "always needed" work is done by the shared expert.

Flag — two quick claims

"Switch was 7 times faster in training and 17 times faster in inference": the page gives only the 7× training figure; the "17×" is not on the page and I could not tie it to the table. The "we use 10% of our brain" analogy: the idea that humans use only 10% of the brain is a popular myth. The useful part of the analogy is that different parts of the brain are active for different tasks, which is sparse activation. He also compared network depth to the brain's many backward connections and mentioned DeepSeek's "mHC" with four residual channels; this was a side remark, not lesson content.

Carry forward

MoE reaches the quality of a much larger dense model for the compute of a small one. It pays in memory and communication.

11. The total-to-active ratio keeps growing

Kimi K2 measured this directly. It defines sparsity as total experts divided by active experts. At the same active size, sparsity 48 reached the same validation loss (1.5) with 1.69× fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8. It stopped at 48 because larger values made the infrastructure harder to run.

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash also carries 196B parameters of Engram, a separate lookup memory addressed by short token sequences. It is added at two layers and never goes through the router, so it is counted apart from the experts.

He showed the page's interactive widget: raising the number of experts raises total parameters but barely changes compute per token (only the router grows). Raising the number chosen per token raises compute.

12. The router: softmax or sigmoid

The router multiplies the token vector by its matrix to get one raw number (a logit) per expert. A score function turns logits into scores; the top k are kept and divided by their sum so the kept weights add up to 1.

Jargon — softmax, sigmoid, softplus

Softmax computes ez for each expert and divides by the sum over all experts, so scores compete and add up to 1. Sigmoid computes 1/(1 + e−z) for each expert on its own, giving a number between 0 and 1 with no competition. Softplus is log(1 + ez), a smooth version of ReLU; DeepSeek-V4 uses √softplus(z).

The lesson's example: 8 experts, k = 2, and the same logits as the token "is" in the snake diagram.

Who uses what: the reference model uses softmax over all 128, keeps 8 and rescales. DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2, GLM-4.5 and MiMo-V2.6 use sigmoid; DeepSeek-V4 uses √softplus. A study of balancing methods found sigmoid less sensitive to uneven load. He did not pick one for V5: "we have to test it".

Routed scaling factor. Some models multiply the kept weights by a constant (2.5 in DeepSeek-V3, 1.5 in DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash). With many small experts each weight is small, and the factor restores the size of the layer's output.

Two stability rules. Run the router in 32-bit: Switch's base model diverged in 16-bit, and casting only the router to 32-bit fixed it while staying faster than full 32-bit (1,390 vs 1,160 examples per second). Start the router small: Switch initialised weights at one tenth of the usual scale, which made runs consistent.

Flag — softmax formula and the "which is better" message

The transcript describes softmax as "ez divided by 1 + ez", which is the sigmoid formula. His comments then argued that softmax keeps the router's preference better ("sigmoid destroys the preference structure"), while the page reports that sigmoid is less sensitive to uneven load and that most new models use sigmoid. Both are true at once: sigmoid flattens the preference (0.507 vs 0.493), and that flattening is one reason it balances more easily. He also said "Flash 4.1" multiplies by 2.5; the page gives 2.5 for DeepSeek-V3 and V4-Pro, and 1.5 for V4.1-Flash. The widget numbers he read out ("503 and 152", "796 and 231") are from an interactive token not reproduced on the page, so I cannot check them.

Carry forward

One matrix product → scores → top k → weights that add to 1. Keep the router in fp32 and start it small.

13. Many small experts, and the shared expert

Early MoEs used a few big experts. Current ones use many small ones, because the number of possible combinations explodes.

DeepSeekMoE (January 2024) named two problems with a few large experts: each must hold very different knowledge, and several end up learning the same common knowledge. Its two fixes:

  • Fine-grained experts. Split each expert into m smaller ones and choose m times as many, so compute stays the same. 16 experts choose 2 gives 120 combinations; split each into 4 (64 experts, choose 8) gives 4,426,165,368.
  • Shared expert. One expert used by every token gives common knowledge one place to live. In DeepSeekMoE's 2B model, removing the shared expert (and adding one more routed expert at the same compute) raised the loss on the Pile from 1.808 to 2.414.
Jargon — the Pile (dataset)

A public 800 GB collection of English text built by EleutherAI in 2020 from 22 sources: web pages, books, arXiv papers, GitHub code, PubMed medical abstracts, Wikipedia, legal texts, forum posts and more. "Loss on the Pile" means how well a model predicts the next token on held-out Pile text; lower is better.

Expert width as a fraction of hidden size has fallen: Mixtral 3.5, Llama 4 1.6, reference model 0.375, DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2 0.286, Qwen3-Next and Qwen3.5 0.25. Krajewski et al. found experts the size of a full FFN "not optimal at almost any computational budget". On shared experts the field is split: DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2, GLM and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash use one; the reference model, gpt-oss, MiniMax-M2 and MiMo-V2.6 use none; the OLMoE study found no benefit in its setting. He said he likes a shared expert as a "manager" that is always on, and connected it to the always-on data idea from an earlier session.

14. What experts actually learn

Researchers traced which tokens reach which experts. The result: experts group tokens by kind (punctuation, verbs, numbers, names, code indentation), much more than by subject (biology vs physics).

Jargon — GSM8K, refusal rate, "arXiv / biology / philosophy papers"

GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) is a benchmark of about 8,500 grade-school maths word problems written by people; the model must reach the right final number, and the score is the percentage solved. Refusal rate is the share of harmful requests a model declines; SAFEx measures it on a set of harmful prompts. The Mixtral analysis used subsets of the Pile (Section 13): ArXiv (physics/maths/CS preprints), PubMed Abstracts (biology/medicine) and PhilPapers (philosophy). The page does not name the Python benchmark used by MONET, or the benchmarks behind REAP's "under 2%".

He then went on a side topic: SAFEx's finding is how people make "uncensored" (abliterated) versions of open models, by finding and switching off the experts or directions that cause refusals. He argued practitioners should understand this, for example for security work that default models refuse.

Reading two rows together. He asked the class to combine "routing is 60% settled after 1% of training" with "half the experts can be removed". His reading: the router is forced to decide very early, before embeddings are good, so it settles on surface patterns of language, and many experts end up near-copies of each other. His proposed fix is to start with few experts and grow the count as training goes, which leads to Section 20.

Carry forward

Experts mostly learn kinds of tokens. A few matter hugely; many can be removed.

15. Load imbalance and routing collapse

The router and the experts train together, which creates a loop that feeds on itself:

He added a warning that applies well beyond MoE: sending all tokens to the best expert lowers the loss quickly, but we want the lowest final loss, so the router must sometimes be pushed away from its short-term favourite.

Measured cases: with no balancing, the busiest expert got 17.8× the average load (Shazeer et al., 2017). OLMoE without a balancing loss initially sent every first-layer token to one expert. MiMo-V2.6, during reinforcement learning with a trainable router: within 20 steps the busiest expert went from 6× to 16× the average and nearly-unused experts from 0.5% to 22%. Restoring only the old router weights fixed it, so the team froze the router for all of reinforcement learning. Imbalance also slows training, because every GPU waits for the busiest one; in MiMo one GPU got over 30× the average tokens and ran out of memory.

MaxVio = (max load − average load) ÷ average load 0 = perfectly even. 1 = the busiest expert has twice the average.
Flag — MiMo numbers

The transcript says nearly-unused experts went "from 5.5 to 22%"; the page says from 0.5% to 22%.

16. Capacity and token dropping (the old fix)

The first fix gave each expert a fixed number of slots per batch. A token that arrives after its expert is full is dropped: it skips that expert and carries on through the residual connection.

His class example: 10 experts and 1,000 tokens. On average each expert should get 100. If expert 1 already has 100 and token 101 arrives, it is dropped.

Switch used factors 1.0 and 1.25 and dropped under 1% of tokens. Fixed shapes made the computation simple. But dropping costs quality: MegaBlocks (2022) rewrote the expert computation as block-sparse matrix multiplication so any load runs in one pass. Its dropless model cut validation loss by 0.26 where capacity factor 1.0 cut it by 0.15. DeepSeek-V3, OLMoE and the reference model drop nothing, and a 2026 study of over 2,000 runs found dropless routing consistently better. His summary: if you see "capacity factor 1.25" in a paper, you are reading the old design.

Flag — what "dropped" means, and the widget numbers

He said dropped tokens "are sent to somebody else forcefully". In the capacity method a dropped token goes to no expert for that slot; it passes through the residual connection unchanged. (Rerouting overflow to another expert is a different, less common method.) He also read "expert 0 got 818 tokens, capacity 64, so 54 were dropped" from the widget; 818 − 64 = 754, so at least one of these numbers is mis-heard.

17. Auxiliary loss (the second fix)

Add a second, small term to the training loss that is low when load is even and high when a few experts take most tokens. Switch Transformer's version:

Laux = α · N · Σi fi · Pi N = number of experts; fi = share of tokens sent to expert i; Pi = average router probability for expert i.

f has no gradient (picking the top k is a step, with no slope), but P does. Pushing the product down lowers the router's probabilities for busy experts and raises them for idle ones. Worked with N = 128 and α = 0.01: perfectly even (every f = P = 1/128) gives 0.01; everything on one expert (f = P = 1) gives 1.28.

He explained α with numbers: if the next-token loss is 5 and the raw balance loss is 4, adding them gives 9 and training would focus on balance. With α = 0.1 the total is 5.4. In the course's earlier model the team kept α in a JSON file that training re-reads, so they could raise it live ("hot-plug") when the router misbehaved. Switch tested α from 0.1 to 0.00001 and chose 0.01; the reference model's config uses 0.001 (confirmed in its config.json).

Router z-loss (from ST-MoE) is a second extra loss, for stability rather than balance. It penalises large logits, because large logits cause rounding errors inside the exponentials. With coefficient 0.001 all three test runs stayed stable, against four of six without it.

Flag — α values in the transcript

"Switch picked from 0.1 to 0.001": the page says 0.1 to 0.00001. "DeepSeek uses 0.001": the 0.001 on the page is the reference model's (Qwen3) coefficient; DeepSeek-V3 uses the bias method (next section) plus a tiny sequence loss of 0.0001.

Carry forward

An auxiliary loss balances through the router's gradient, and that gradient fights the language loss.

18. Auxiliary-loss-free balancing (the current fix)

This is the method he said V5 will use. Each expert gets one extra number, a bias, kept outside the loss. The bias is added to the score only when choosing the top k. The weight that multiplies the expert's output still comes from the original score. After each step, busy experts' biases go down a little and idle experts' biases go up a little.

Worked example (the router from Section 12, sigmoid scores). Expert 5 has been too busy and has a bias of −0.5. For choosing, its score becomes 0.858 − 0.5 = 0.358, so expert 2 (0.769) is chosen instead. Expert 0 keeps its weight from its original score.

Evidence. Wang et al. (2024) found γ = 0.001 best. At 1B parameters: auxiliary loss gave perplexity 9.56 with MaxVio 0.72; the bias method gave 9.50 with 0.04. At 3B: 7.97 / 0.52 vs 7.92 / 0.04. Better loss and better balance. DeepSeek-V3 used γ = 0.001 for the first 14.3T tokens and 0 for the last 500B, plus a tiny sequence-level loss (0.0001) to stop extreme imbalance inside one sequence. GLM-4.5, Kimi K2, DeepSeek-V4, Ling and MiMo use this recipe. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash keeps separate biases for text and image tokens, because balancing the combined load can hide an imbalance within one kind.

Lightning LM (the instructor's 2026 model family) used the sign rule at 5B and 9B. At 120B (460 experts) it used a three-tier controller that corrects harder when an expert leaves a target band, and its update speed was halved from 0.0001 to 0.00005 at step 732 after it over-corrected.

He told a cautionary story: in the previous course model, a student implemented an auxiliary-loss router instead of the agreed loss-free one, and about two days of compute were wasted before the team noticed the router was failing.

Jargon — perplexity

e raised to the average loss. It reads as "how many equally likely next tokens the model is choosing between". Lower is better; 9.50 vs 9.56 is a small but real gain at 1B.

Flag — Lightning LM and V4.1-Flash numbers

Transcript: "update speed halved from 0.01 to 0.001 by 7 6 3 7 52 steps". Page: from 0.0001 to 0.00005 at step 732. Transcript: V4.1-Flash biases update at "0.01 each"; page: 0.001. Carry forward

A per-expert bias used only for choosing balances load without touching the language loss. This is the current standard and the V5 choice.

19. Balancing scope: count over what?

Any balancing method counts load over some group of tokens: one sequence, one GPU's micro-batch, or the whole batch across all GPUs. The smaller the group, the harder the rule.

The Qwen team (2025) measured it. Most frameworks counted load per micro-batch, which holds only a few sequences, so the rule was applied almost sequence by sequence. Summing the counts across GPUs first changed the results:

20. Growing an MoE from a dense model

This is the part the assignment tests. An MoE need not start from random weights. A dense model that already works can be turned into one (upcycling), and an MoE with few experts can be grown into one with many. He called this "the core journey of training a model": start with a dense ("linear") model to get the embeddings right cheaply, then grow.

Jargon — "neuron" here

One neuron of an FFN = one row of the up (and gate) matrix plus the matching column of the down matrix. "Slicing" or "partitioning" an FFN means taking a subset of these neurons. Because the non-linearity acts on each neuron separately, the FFN's output is exactly the sum of what each neuron slice produces (A2 checks this numerically).

Qwen2's recipe for the reference shape: 6,144 cuts into only 8 pieces of 768, but 128 experts are needed, so make about 16 copies, shuffle each copy's neurons, cut into experts, and redraw half of each expert's weights. Upcycling saves compute only up to a point: an MoE from scratch caught up after about 120% of the dense budget in the original study and 25% in OLMoE's. Growing the expert count. The Lightning LM path, and "collapse along clone families"

An earlier attempt cloned experts without redrawing any neurons. With hard top-k routing, dead experts rose from 27 to 38, 122, 154 and 168 out of 460 within a few hundred steps. The router could not tell near-identical clones apart, so each family of 23 clones collapsed onto a few members. The fix was probabilistic selection early in training.

At step 4,000 the 120B model had zero dead experts, a busiest expert at about 0.5% of the load, and a top-10 share of 4.09% against an even share of 2.17%. The Lightning LM report states the principle as "preserve the function before the router specializes".

Carry forward

Grow by copying or slicing the dense FFN, make the copies different, and protect new experts (probabilistic selection) until the router can tell them apart.

21. Expert parallelism: tokens travel to experts

The reference model's experts hold 28.99B parameters, too many for one GPU to train. Expert parallelism puts different experts on different GPUs; on one 8-GPU node (EP = 8), each GPU holds 16 of the 128 experts per layer. Attention, router and embeddings are copied on every GPU.

  1. Attention and the router run at home.
  2. Look up the fixed map (expert ÷ 16) to find each expert's GPU.
  3. Count how many tokens go to each GPU, and exchange just these counts first, so every GPU can set aside space.
  4. Dispatch: an all-to-all sends the token vectors.
  5. Each GPU runs its 16 experts on whatever arrived, as one grouped matrix multiplication.
  6. Combine: results return by the same paths; the home GPU puts them back in order and adds the 8 weighted outputs.

The traffic. Each token sends 8 copies of its vector: 8 × 2,048 × 2 bytes = 32,768 bytes, and the same again to combine. With 8 GPUs, 7/8 of it leaves the GPU. For one 8,192-token sequence per GPU, forward and backward, across 48 layers: 45.1 GB (recomputed). That takes 0.050 s on B200 NVLink, 0.100 s on H100 NVLink, and 0.90 s over one network card between nodes. The compute for the same sequence is about 0.073 s on a B200. So inside a node, communication is about as long as compute and the two must overlap; between nodes it is 12× the compute. Hence Megatron-LM's rule: keep EP inside one node. Each expert receives on average 8 × 8,192 × 8 ÷ 128 = 4,096 tokens per step, well above the roughly 280 needed to keep a B200's matrix units busy.

Flag — "these are heads of the token"

Explaining the widget, he said the dots are "heads" of one token. On the page, each dot is one copy of a token going to one of its 8 chosen experts (colour = destination GPU, digit = home GPU). They are not attention heads.

22. Expert parallelism with ZeRO, and the memory bill

The two kinds of weights are split differently. Dense parts (attention, router, embeddings: 1.54B) are copied on every GPU with ZeRO-1 dividing their optimizer state. Experts are already divided by EP, so each expert exists on exactly one GPU and needs no gradient averaging when EP equals the GPU count.

Other tools mentioned: Megatron's MoE Parallel Folding (attention and experts use different GPU groupings; Mixtral 8x22B on 128 H100s went from 46.3% to 49.3% utilisation); DeepSeek-V3's DualPipe (one micro-batch's all-to-all runs while another computes); and DeepSeek's EPLB for serving (extra copies of the busiest experts on spare GPUs, re-chosen about every 10 minutes).

He gave a practical story from Lightning LM: 460 experts on 8 GPUs is 57.5 per GPU, so some GPUs held one more expert than others. The number 460 came from 20 × 23, chosen to fill memory, and in hindsight a count divisible by 8 would have been cleaner.

Flag — two numbers

460 ÷ 8 = 57.5, not "57.1". He also mentioned EP = 32 costing "0.77 s" of communication; that value is from the page's widget and is not in the page text.

24. Choosing a layout, and V5 decisions

The page gives a fixed order: (1) count total and active; (2) set EP to the GPUs in one node; (3) ZeRO-1 for the dense parts; (4) check training state per GPU; (5) add activations; (6) check tokens per expert (a few hundred or more); (7) compare all-to-all time with compute; (8) cross nodes only if it does not fit; (9) measure the step time and let the measurement choose. For the reference model: one B200 node fits (87.4 of 167.6 GiB), one H100 node does not (74.5 GiB), two H100 nodes with EP = 16 fit (59.3 GiB). Megatron-LM publishes exactly this config: EP 8, no TP, no PP, recomputing the expert activation and layer norm.

V5 decisions stated in class
  • Balancing: per-expert bias for choosing (γ ≈ 0.001), a tiny sequence loss (≈ 0.0001), load counted over the whole batch.
  • No dropped tokens: dropless routing with grouped expert computation.
  • EP = 8 inside one node, ZeRO-1 for the dense parts.
  • Router in fp32, frozen during reinforcement learning.
  • Still open: dense or MoE, sizes, from scratch or grown, number/width of experts and shared expert, H100 or B200.

Flag — what "show they continue to train" needs

A2. The one idea that decides everything: does conversion keep the function?

At the moment of conversion, the MoE either computes the same thing as the dense model, or something different. If it computes something different, the loss jumps up at the switch and must recover before it can improve. The Lightning LM report states this as its rule: "preserve the function before the router specializes".

Your session 13 FFN is 320 → 1,280 → 320 with GELU. Its 1,280 inner neurons can be cut into slices. I checked numerically: the sum of 8 slices of 160 equals the full FFN output to 1.6 × 10−15 (floating-point noise). But if only 2 of the 8 slices run, the output is 83% off.

So there are two families of conversion. Function-preserving ones (copy, or shared expert = the dense FFN) start at the dense model's loss exactly. Function-changing ones (plain partition with top-k, drop-upcycling) start higher and recover. Both are valid for the brief; they tell different stories. This is the heart of Decision 2.

A3. The plan, step by step

  4. Step 4 Continue training the MoE with balancing; log loss, per-expert load, MaxVio, dead experts.
  5. Step 5 Continue the dense model for the same tokens, from the same checkpoint.

Because each family starts as 8 identical clones, it is exactly the "clone family" setting from Section 20, so early probabilistic selection or a little neuron redrawing would be needed.

Why I recommend E: a fair test needs equal work per token

The whole claim of MoE is: store more parameters, do the same work per token, get a lower loss. To test that claim, the MoE must do the same work per token as the dense model it is compared with. Otherwise, if it wins, you cannot tell whether the win came from MoE or simply from doing more arithmetic.

How E starts from exactly the dense model. The dense FFN's output equals the sum of its 4 neuron slices of 320 (A2 showed this is exact). Make 8 copies of each slice: 32 experts in 4 "families". At the start, the router picks one expert from each family, with weight 1 each. Every family contributes one copy of its slice, so the four add up to the dense output exactly. (Weight 1 for each of 4 experts is simply a routed scaling factor of 4, the same idea as DeepSeek's 2.5 in Section 12.)

The costs of E, honestly:

  • A non-standard start. "One per family" is a constraint I am adding to keep the function exact. It must be relaxed later to normal top-4, and relaxing it changes the function slightly, so expect a small bump at that point. When to relax it is a small extra decision.
  • Clone families. The 8 copies in a family start identical, which is exactly the Lightning LM "collapse along clone families" failure (Section 20). It needs early probabilistic selection or a little neuron redrawing, and a dead-expert count to prove it worked.
  • More code and more to debug than A.

Why not A, if it is simpler? A is a perfectly valid answer to the brief: the loss is continuous and keeps falling. It just cannot answer "did MoE help?", because A does twice the FFN work per token. Why not B? Each token uses a quarter of the FFN, so it will lose to the dense model; it shows recovery, not benefit. Why not D? Drop-upcycling is the published best at large scale, but it changes the function at the switch, and with top-2 it has A's compute problem.

Open — the conversion (highest-stakes decision)

My recommendation: E, because at equal active compute any loss gain is due to the extra stored parameters, which is the whole claim of MoE. It is the most instructive, but also the most work. A is the safe fallback: exact, simple, and a clear continuous curve, but it costs 1.6× the dense compute per token, so a win over dense is less meaningful. A two-arm run (for example A and D, or E with and without grouped start) doubles the MoE training time. Which do you want: one arm or two, and which?

Open — which layers

Convert all 16 FFNs (like the reference model), or leave the first one or two dense (some models do this because first-layer routing settles slowly)? Recommendation: all 16, to keep one variable.

A7. Decision 4: token budget and learning-rate schedule

A subtle trap: session 13's schedule decays the learning rate (cosine) to 10% by the end. If you convert at the end of such a run, the MoE starts at a tiny learning rate and barely moves, which would look like "it stopped learning". Two clean ways out:

  • Warmup-stable-decay (recommended). Hold a constant learning rate for most of the dense run, convert while it is still high, and decay only at the end of each continuation (MoE and control get the same schedule).
  • Re-warm. Finish the dense cosine, then give the MoE a short warmup back to a higher rate. This causes a loss bump of its own, which mixes with any conversion effect.
Decided 27 Sep — budget like session 13

Every phase uses session 13's settings: 50M tokens, batch 32 × 512, learning rate 1e-3 with about 200 warm-up steps, weight decay 0.1, β2 0.95, gradient clip 1.0, Colab T4 in fp16. So: 50M dense, then 50M MoE and 50M dense-control from the same checkpoint. The one change from session 13 is the schedule shape: warmup-stable-decay instead of cosine, for the reason above. Rough time on a T4: about 16 minutes per dense phase at session 13's 53,000 tokens/s, and roughly 25–45 minutes for the MoE (a guess until measured), so about 1 to 1.3 hours in total.

A9. What gets measured, and the gates

  • G1, conversion gate. For preserving options: validation loss right after conversion equals the dense loss within a tight tolerance (fp32 check on a fixed batch, before any MoE step). For changing options: the jump is reported, not hidden.
  • G2, keeps training. MoE validation loss at the end is below its value at conversion, and the fall is larger than the measured step-to-step noise.
  • G3, balance. No dead experts at the end (or the count is reported per layer), and MaxVio is logged over time.
