1. The brief, and what it really asks

Lesson page, section 21

"Train a Linear model and convert that into an MoE! Your call on model size and data trained on, but must show they continue to train and reduce loss!"

What that means in practice

  • "Linear model" = a dense transformer: one feed-forward network (FFN) per layer. The instructor used "linear" for "dense" throughout the session.
  • Train it, convert every FFN into experts plus a router while keeping the trained weights, then keep training.
  • Proof needed: a loss curve that continues across the conversion point and keeps going down.
  • What we add: a dense model that simply keeps training from the same point (the control). If the MoE ends lower than the control, we have measured that MoE helped, not just that it survived.

D6 — The optimiser's memory at conversion: copy it decided

AdamW keeps two running averages for every weight: the recent average gradient (momentum) and the recent average squared gradient (scale). R1 has them for every dense FFN weight; the 32 experts are new tensors. Decision: each expert starts with the averages of the dense slice it was copied from, and keeps R1's step count. The router, which is new, starts with empty averages.

optionproscons
Copy the moments (chosen)the first MoE step moves each expert exactly as the next dense step would have moved that slice; any change in the curve is then caused by the MoE, not by the optimiser; R2 keeps its moments too, so the comparison is faira few more lines in the converter, and a check that the copied state matches
Reset the expert momentssimpleron empty averages Adam's first steps move every weight by about the full learning rate at once; that jolt would show as a loss bump that R2 does not have
Reset everythingsimplestthe same jolt across all 22.5M weights
Why an empty average makes a big first step

Adam divides each gradient by the square root of its recent squared average, so a weight's step is roughly learning rate × (average gradient ÷ typical gradient size). On a fresh start, both averages hold just the first gradient, the ratio is about 1, and every weight moves by about the full learning rate, 0.001. After thousands of steps the averages are smooth and most steps are much smaller. Copying the averages keeps the experts in that calm state.

Why the router is the exception: it has no history to copy. It starts at zero, so all 32 experts score the same, and the first step keeps the dense function whatever the router's first update is.

4. How the conversion works (option E)

Step 1: cut, then copy. The dense FFN's output is exactly the sum of its 4 neuron slices of 320, because GELU acts on each neuron separately. Each slice is copied 8 times, giving 32 experts in 4 families.

Step 2: how the router weights the four chosen experts. The obvious rule, rescaling the chosen weight inside each family to 1, has a hidden flaw. With one pick per family, that weight is always exactly 1, whatever the router says. So the language loss would never send a gradient to the router, and it would never learn. The fix is to rescale the four chosen scores together so they sum to 4, which is a routed scaling factor of 4 (the lesson's Section 12).

In plain terms

The router has two jobs: which expert in each family, and how much each family's expert counts. The bias balancing (lesson Section 18) steers the "which". The weights summing to 4 give the router a "how much" to learn from.

Corrected 29 Sep — "exact" means the same maths, not bit-identical numbers

This section first promised a bit-exact start. That is wrong, and a test found it while preparing v2. The four slices add up to the dense output mathematically, but a computer adds numbers in a different order when it does four small products instead of one big one, and the last digits round differently. On random weights of our shapes the difference is about {{rt_fp32}} relative in fp32 and {{rt_fp64}} in fp64: rounding, not an error. So gate G1 now checks in fp64 (must agree to about 1e-12) and reports the fp32 difference as the rounding floor. See Section 7.

6.5 Why R1 ended so close to session 13, and what we can and cannot conclude

The plan predicted that dropping the learning-rate decay would cost R1 about 0.1–0.3 in final loss. It cost {{gap_s13}}. Three things differ between the two runs, so this is not a clean measurement of "what decay is worth":

  • The schedule. Session 13 decayed (cosine down to 10%); R1 stayed flat. This is the difference the prediction was about.
  • The training text. Session 13 shuffled a 50.5M-token file. R1 took 50M tokens from a shuffled 100.5M-token file, so it saw a different sample of FineWeb, drawn from twice as many documents. The validation set is built the same way, from the same first documents of the stream.
  • Chance. Each run was done once, with one seed. We have not measured how much two otherwise identical runs differ, so a gap of {{gap_s13}} could be partly or wholly noise.
What this does and does not tell us

It does not show that decay is worth only {{gap_s13}}. It does show that a flat learning rate cost little here, and that R1 was still improving at the end ({{last10_drop}} over the last 10M tokens). That is exactly the state we wanted to convert from: a model that can still learn quickly. The value of decay will be measured cleanly in v3: R2 is the same model on the same data with and without its final 20% decay phase, so the drop during that phase is the decay effect.

Why it happens, in plain words

At the switch, the 8 clones in each family are still almost the same, so their router scores differ by tiny amounts. Those differences are nearly the same for every token. While selection is probabilistic, that does not matter: each clone keeps getting its share by chance. Once selection becomes "take the highest", almost every token goes to whichever clone is a hair ahead. That is the instructor's "collapse along clone families" (lesson Section 20). The balancing bias is meant to push back, but at 0.001 per step it needs hundreds of steps to overturn even a small lead, so the pile-up lasts.

8.3 Why, as far as the data can tell

The two failures have different timing, so they probably have different causes.

  • The loss rise (steps 0–300). Three explanations fit, and this run cannot separate them:
    1. Noisy clones. With random selection, each clone trains on a random eighth of the tokens but still takes full-size Adam steps, so the 8 copies wander apart by noise (clone distance 0.35 by step 300). At evaluation each token goes to the clone with the highest score, and that clone has learned from only an eighth of the new data.
    2. A fast router. The router starts fresh with Adam at learning rate 1e-3. Its steps change the four family weights quickly, and those weights are part of the model's function.
    3. No warm-up after conversion. The model was restarted into a new shape at full learning rate.
  • The collapse (after step 300). During the probabilistic phase the router learned strong preferences between clones. Random sampling hid them, because a clone with a high score still did not get every token. Hard selection made them count at once. The router's preferences grew faster than the bias could correct.

Why splitting could hurt, in plain words

Its gradient is a much noisier estimate of where to go. Adam, however, scales every step to about the same size (the learning rate), whatever the noise. So each clone takes full-size steps in noisier directions, and the eight copies wander apart. F's clones were already {{d_cd_F25}} of their size apart after only 25 steps. A smaller learning rate for the experts would shrink the noisy steps. That is exactly what E did by accident in its first 50 steps, when the learning rate was still low.

14.2 Why the decay favoured the dense model (an explanation, not yet tested)

Lowering the learning rate at the end of a run gives a quick drop in loss: the steps shrink, so the model settles into a lower point instead of bouncing around it. R2's FFN was learning at full speed, so the decay turned a large step into a small one. R3's experts had been learning at 1/8 speed for the whole run, so they were already taking small steps. The decay took them down to 1/80 of the base rate, and there was less "bounce" left to remove. R2s, which has the same slowed FFN, also gained less from the decay ({{f_dec_R2s}}). That fits the explanation, but it is inference from three runs, not a measured cause.

What this suggests trying next (not done here)
  • Relax the experts' slowdown over time. Start the experts at 1/8 speed, when they have just been split and each sees only an eighth of the tokens, then raise them towards full speed as the clones specialise. The clone distance grew steadily ({{f_cd}} by the end), so they do become different.
  • A longer run. Phase 2's lead over R2s was still growing when the decay began. A longer stable phase may let the MoE carry a bigger lead into the decay.
  • A faster expert kernel. The 32 small matrix products per layer made R3 {{f_slow}}× slower than dense. A grouped kernel (the lesson's Section 16) would close much of that gap.
